# Executive Summary

This report synthesizes the analysis of global shark encounter records from 2010-2017 to identify trends in geographic hotspot frequency. The primary findings indicate that the **United States (specifically Florida) and Australia are the dominant global hotspots**, together accounting for over 80% of all reliably recorded incidents in 2017. Encounters are most frequently associated with **surface water activities like surfing and swimming**. While patterns in time-of-day are observable, significant **data quality limitations**, including missing species identification in 60% of records and the inclusion of "Provoked" or "Invalid" incidents, constrain the certainty of more granular conclusions. Where species are identified, White sharks are disproportionately linked to fatal outcomes.

# Geographic Hotspot Analysis

Analysis of incident locations reveals a highly concentrated geographic distribution. The following hotspots account for the vast majority of recorded encounters.

*   **Primary Hotspot: USA.** The United States is the single most frequent location for shark encounters, with Florida being the epicenter.
    *   **Confidence: High.** The USA accounted for 34 encounters (61% of the global total in 2017). Of these, 28 incidents (82% of the US total) occurred in Florida, making it the world's most concentrated area for recorded encounters.
*   **Secondary Hotspot: Australia.** Australia represents the second-most significant region for shark encounters.
    *   **Confidence: High.** The country recorded 11 incidents in 2017 (20% of the global total), with events distributed across Western Australia (3), New South Wales (3), Queensland (3), and Victoria (1).
*   **Global Distribution.** Outside of the two primary hotspots, incidents are infrequent and widely scattered.
    *   **Confidence: Medium.** The remaining 19% of incidents were spread across eight other countries. Of note are Reunion Island, which recorded two fatal encounters, and the Bahamas, with three non-fatal incidents.
*   **Geographic Species Correlation.** The type of shark involved often correlates with the geographic region.
    *   **Confidence: Medium.** Encounters in Australia frequently involved White sharks, which were also associated with 3 of the 5 total fatal incidents recorded globally. Encounters in Florida rarely had a confirmed species identification but included mentions of bull and tiger sharks.

# Temporal Trends and Activity Patterns

The timing of encounters and the activities of the individuals involved show clear, high-confidence patterns that correlate with human water usage.

*   **High-Risk Activities.** The majority of encounters involve people engaged in activities on or near the water's surface.
    *   **Confidence: High.** Surfing was the most common activity, linked to 23 incidents (41% of the total). This was followed by swimming (8 incidents, 14%) and body boarding (4 incidents, 7%). These activities present a silhouette from below and involve splashing, which are known to attract sharks.
*   **Time of Day.** Encounters are distributed across daylight hours, with apparent peaks that align with popular recreation times.
    *   **Confidence: Medium.** Where time was recorded, incidents occurred between 06:00 and 19:00. Clusters were observed during mid-morning (09:00-12:00) and mid-afternoon (14:00-17:00).
*   **Seasonal Trends.** The data suggests a potential concentration during warmer months, but the evidence is insufficient for a firm conclusion.
    *   **Confidence: Low.** Available records hint at higher encounter rates from March to October in the Northern Hemisphere, but a lack of consistent date reporting across all records prevents a definitive seasonal analysis.

# Data Quality and Limitations

The analysis is significantly constrained by the quality and consistency of the source data. These limitations must be considered when interpreting the findings.

*   **Pervasive Documentation Gaps.** Critical information is missing from a majority of the records, which directly impacts the depth of analysis.
    *   **Confidence: High.** Key data gaps include:
        *   **Species:** The shark species was not identified in 60% of records.
        *   **Time:** Precise time of day was absent in 40% of cases.
        *   **Injury Detail:** Descriptions of injuries are inconsistent, ranging from clinical to anecdotal.
*   **Inclusion of Non-Standard Incidents.** The dataset mixes unprovoked encounters with other incident types, complicating the analysis of natural shark behavior.
    *   **Confidence: High.** The raw data includes records explicitly labeled "Provoked" (e.g., interacting with a hooked shark) and "Invalid" (e.g., shark involvement was ruled out). These records skew counts and must be filtered to assess true unprovoked encounter rates.
*   **Geographic Reporting Bias.** The high number of incidents in the USA and Australia may be influenced by more robust and accessible reporting systems compared to other regions.
    *   **Confidence: Medium.** The extreme concentration of data from these two countries likely reflects a combination of actual encounter frequency and superior incident reporting infrastructure. Encounter rates in other coastal nations may be significantly underreported.

# Recommendations for Future Analysis

Based on the limitations identified in the current dataset, the following steps are recommended to produce a more accurate and insightful analysis:

1.  **Perform Data Cleansing.** A crucial next step is to create a filtered dataset that contains only confirmed, unprovoked encounters. Records labeled "Invalid," "Provoked," or "Boat" should be segregated to allow for a focused analysis of unprovoked incidents, which will yield more accurate risk and trend assessments.
2.  **Focus on High-Density Regions.** Conduct a deeper, region-specific analysis for Florida and Australia. This analysis should aim to correlate the cleaned encounter data with local variables such as water temperature, tidal patterns, tourist seasons, and specific beach population data to better understand the environmental and human factors driving the high incident rates.
3.  **Standardize Injury and Species Data.** For ongoing data collection, a standardized classification system for injury severity (e.g., minor, moderate, severe, fatal) should be implemented. Efforts should also be made to improve species identification, potentially by cross-referencing investigator notes with regional species distribution data, to strengthen the analysis of species-specific behavior and risk.
