### **Shark Encounter Analysis Report: 2017 Incidents**

This report synthesizes the analysis of 10 documented shark-human encounters from 2017. The findings are based on a structured review of incident records, focusing on geographic distribution, human activity, injury outcomes, and data quality.

---

### **Executive Summary**

An analysis of 10 shark encounter records from 2017 reveals several key patterns. All documented incidents were non-fatal. Encounters were geographically concentrated, with the USA (4 incidents) and Australia (3 incidents) accounting for 70% of the total. Surfing was the most common activity associated with an encounter. While half of the incidents resulted in minor injuries like lacerations, a concerning 30% led to severe harm. Data quality is a significant challenge; half of all records lack specific details on the shark species involved, and many are missing information on the time of day and the age of the person involved. These data gaps limit the depth of possible analysis and highlight a need for improved data collection standards.

---

### **Key Findings**

This section details the primary conclusions drawn from the 2017 incident data.

*   **Geographic Distribution:** Encounters were recorded across five countries. The United States had the most incidents (4), with three of those occurring in Florida. Australia followed with three incidents in three different states.
    *   **Confidence: High**

*   **Survival Rate and Injury Severity:** All 10 encounters were non-fatal. However, the severity of injuries varied significantly:
    *   Two incidents (20%) resulted in no physical injury to the person.
    *   Five incidents (50%) caused minor injuries, such as lacerations or abrasions.
    *   Three incidents (30%) resulted in severe injuries, including a severed forearm and a severely bitten leg.
    *   **Confidence: High**

*   **Activity During Encounters:** Surface water sports were most common. Surfing was the activity in three incidents, and spearfishing in two. Two incidents involved direct interaction with sharks (feeding and touching).
    *   **Confidence: High**

*   **Encounter Type:** Of the nine incidents where a "Type" was assigned, eight (89%) were classified as "Unprovoked." The single "Provoked" incident involved a person touching a hooked shark.
    *   **Confidence: High**

*   **Species and Injury Correlation:** A potential link between shark species and injury severity was observed, though the small sample size requires caution.
    *   The two encounters involving identified Tiger sharks both resulted in significant injuries.
    *   Encounters involving an identified White shark and a 7-gill shark resulted in no injuries to the person.
    *   **Confidence: Medium**

*   **Regional Injury Patterns:** The data suggests a possible variation in injury rates by location. All three incidents in Florida resulted in an injury, whereas two of the three incidents in Australia did not.
    *   **Confidence: Medium**

---

### **Data Quality Assessment**

The reliability of the analysis is constrained by significant gaps in the source data. Consistent, complete data is essential for drawing more robust conclusions.

*   **Missing Information:** The dataset suffers from incomplete records.
    *   **Species:** 50% of records lack a clear identification of the shark species.
    *   **Time:** 50% of records are missing the time of the incident.
    *   **Age:** 30% of records do not include the age of the person involved.
    *   **Source:** 20% of records do not cite an investigator or source.
    *   **Type:** 10% of records (one incident) are missing a classification for the encounter type (e.g., Provoked/Unprovoked).
    *   **Confidence: High**

---

### **Limitations and Biases**

The following factors may influence the findings and should be considered when interpreting the results.

*   **Classification Ambiguity:** The definitions used in the data are not always clear-cut. For example, an incident involving "Feeding sharks" was classified as "Unprovoked," despite the deliberate interaction. Similarly, injury descriptions are not standardized, making direct comparisons difficult.
    *   **Confidence: Medium**

*   **Potential Reporting Bias:** The dataset may not represent all shark encounters that occurred.
    *   Severe incidents are more likely to be reported than minor ones, potentially skewing the perception of risk.
    *   The geographic focus on English-speaking countries may reflect differences in reporting infrastructure rather than the true global distribution of incidents.
    *   **Confidence: Medium**

---

### **Recommendations**

To improve the quality and analytical value of future shark encounter data, the following steps are recommended.

1.  **Improve Data Collection:**
    *   Implement mandatory data fields for critical information such as **Time**, **Age**, and **Species**.
    *   Establish a standardized scale for classifying **Injury Severity** to allow for more accurate comparisons.
    *   Develop and enforce clearer guidelines for classifying encounters as **Provoked vs. Unprovoked**, particularly for activities involving intentional interaction like feeding or chumming.

2.  **Enhance Future Analysis:**
    *   With more complete data over multiple years, develop species-specific risk profiles to better understand behavioral patterns.
    *   Investigate potential seasonal or time-of-day patterns once data completeness improves.
    *   Analyze the high injury rate observed in Florida compared to other regions to identify contributing factors.

3.  **Strengthen Quality Assurance:**
    *   Create data validation rules to automatically flag and review inconsistent entries (e.g., an activity like "Feeding sharks" listed as "Unprovoked").
    *   Require source documentation for all incident records to ensure traceability and allow for independent verification.
